# 2026 AI for Humanity Report: key findings

Knowledge file for Fast Forward coaches. Source: Bitner-Laird, L., Wrenn, J., Cavalier, T., Nelson, K., & Davenport, N. (2026). *2026 AI for Humanity Report.* Fast Forward. https://www.ffwd.org/ai-for-humanity-report.pdf. Figures below are the report's own; cite them as "the 2026 AI for Humanity Report found." Never adjust one.

**Who was surveyed.** 119 AI-powered nonprofits (APNs): organizations that use AI to deliver their products, programs, or services, making AI central to their impact. 307 organizations screened, 162 completed, 119 met the APN definition. Plus 20 interviews (10 APNs, 10 AI funders). 46% of respondents were Fast Forward portfolio organizations. Purposive and convenience sample; self-reported; the report says the findings do not generalize to the sector as a whole and should not be read as evidence that AI caused the reported changes. A coach that cites a number carries that caveat.

## What APNs look like

- 59% have 10 or fewer employees; 12% have 51 or more.
- 49% operate on less than $1M a year; 35% on $1M to $4.9M.
- 70% adopted AI after launching; 30% built the nonprofit with AI from the start.
- Just over six in ten run their AI on less than $150K a year.

## What they build with it

- Most common applications: research, data analysis, and knowledge retrieval (75%); directly serving beneficiaries (73%); chatbots and conversational AI (71%); data extraction and document processing (54%).
- Top mission uses: faster or more efficient services (79%); insights for program design (70%).
- Most use AI in several ways at once rather than one tool for one task.

## Delivery

- 92% say AI made service delivery more efficient.
- 55% say AI made personalized services at scale possible.
- 41% use AI to deliver in new languages and formats.
- The clearest gains APNs attribute to AI are in the depth of their work, not its reach. Many reached more people this year; few credit AI alone for it.
- APNs do not adopt technology for its own sake: they identify a specific community or organizational need first, then ask whether AI is the right tool.

## Investment

- 90% of APNs piloting or scaling an AI solution have a plan to scale; only 24% have the resources to execute it.
- 45% name rising costs of AI tools and infrastructure as the top external threat to their AI work.
- 77% name multi-year, unrestricted funding as what would help most.
- Asked what they most wish funders would do: 78% named funding long-term technical infrastructure; 60% chose flexible, unrestricted funding. Those two led every other option by a wide margin.
- Philanthropy typically funds exploration and pilots; it is less willing to fund maintenance and scale. Post-launch costs (testing, monitoring, computing, licensing) rise with usage.

## Capacity

- 64% name a champion who drives the vision as a top enabler of AI adoption. The top enablers all relate to human capacity.
- 72% build their tools in-house on top of existing models or frameworks.
- 55% customize off-the-shelf tools with their own data.
- 63% want to collaborate on accessing technical expertise they do not have in-house.
- Dependency on outside models persists partly because building alternatives takes time; concerns include data privacy and ownership of intellectual property.

## Values and risk

- 88% are concerned about delivering inaccurate information; 84% about exposing or misusing personal data; 66% worry AI could erode trust with the people they serve.
- 76% collect ongoing feedback from the people they serve; 66% test their AI tools directly with beneficiaries before launch.
- Of eleven risks asked about, APNs registered the most concern about potential harms to their communities.
- Not one of the 119 said AI's risks outweigh its benefits or questioned whether AI is an appropriate path for social impact work.
- Governance, monitoring, and communication with beneficiaries require ongoing effort; few grants fund that work.

## The report's recommendations

For nonprofits: keep asking why AI; name what stays human and be transparent about AI use; budget for endurance and price the hidden labor (monitoring, testing, iteration are where the money goes); diversify funding deliberately, not desperately; design feedback loops that include community voices; contribute to the shared commons.

For funders: fund the full lifecycle, not just the launch; fund the people behind the product; build capacity for the shared commons (expertise, tools, standards, data); develop your own AI literacy.

## Context the report sets

Nonprofits are the most trusted institutions in the U.S., ahead of small businesses, courts, government, news media, and corporations, and that trust crosses political lines. At the same time, public skepticism toward AI is rising. APNs take on that tension directly: reputational risk, funder hesitancy, and community skepticism, on top of the risks of the technology itself.
